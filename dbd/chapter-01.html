Chapter 1

Daggers ’n David

The shadows in the room reached out at improbable angles across the wood floor. That suited David just fine. Considering the trouble he already had to take to avoid betrayal by creaking floorboards, having his choice of patches of darkness to dart into felt like a gift.

The old man was still drifting off by the fire. It was his near-mad decoration sense that had split up the shadows in this place that way. Mirror after mirror collected the light from the dying fire in front of him, sending it in random-seeming directions but leaving the better part of the house unilluminated. The half-empty bottle in his hand should have made his personality make more sense, but it didn’t. David had spent plenty of time researching his target, just as he had been taught. He knew him inside and out. Liquor had nothing to do with the decor, the lifestyle, or a dozen other mystifying decisions the old man regularly made. He was just like that.

The old man’s head nodded a few times, then hung down until his ragged beard folded against his chest. In the shadow of a non-functional grandfather clock, David knew not to trust appearances.

If he was actually asleep, he’d drop the bottle.

There were rules for this sort of thing. Guidelines, at least. A hand grasping something might allow for the hand’s owner to be slightly asleep, but slightly asleep was still somewhat aware. Jobs were best completed quickly enough to avoid complications, but that speed had to trade off against safety. David hadn’t alerted any guards on his way in. The lock had picked as smoothly as if it was greased, mostly because he had actually greased it. Rogues didn’t write many books, and they wrote even fewer honest books. The small handful of legitimate-looking tomes he had collected all seemed to agree that this was a time to wait.

So he waited. The old man’s head couldn’t droop deeper, but it dipped heavier and heavier against his ribs as he got further and further from consciousness. David waited until the balance tipped away from waiting in favor of moving, darting from shadow to shadow again once it was clear the old man was either sleeping like a rock or faking the entire thing anyway.

He had been working this target for months, and it had never felt this close. A board creaked very slightly under one of his feet as he moved, but so faintly he couldn’t imagine anyone hearing it any further from the source than he was. He shifted his weight subtly before completing that step, quieting the nightingale board in a fraction of an instant as he slipped into position directly behind the old man’s chair.

Like all the things the elderly target seemed to own, the chair had seen better days. It was probably once a sturdy dependable kind of thing. Decades later, the fact that it was still standing almost qualified as a magic trick. It had been splintered, broken, glued, rebroken, and refinished with some sort of suspicious remnant oil until it was more scars than intact bits, but it was still going. Even better, it was a thin-backed affair. The man had fallen asleep in his own coat, and his pockets were beckoning from well past any interfering chair edge, begging to be picked.

David didn’t have to hold his breath for what came next. He had been holding it for over half a minute already. His hand snaked forward, passing the mouth of the pockets without so much as scraping the edges of the fabric. His fingers hit cold wood, slipping slightly over a beveled edge before a stronger, rougher hand clamped over his wrist.

“Gotcha.” The old man said. “Not a bad try, though.”

“You weren’t asleep.”

“I was. It was the board.” The old man pinned down the box with his free hand before jerking David’s hand out of his pocket and shoving him backwards to the floor. David would have never got away with the box anyway, but it was deeply consistent with everything he knew about the old rogue in front of him to take no unnecessary chances. “Quite the racket. If you hadn't hit that, you might have got me.”

“I doubt it.” David stood up. “You felt my hand in your pocket. I felt you feel it.”

“That I did. But the dice weren’t with you, I think.”

It was true. David’s skills were nothing next to beginner rogue’s, let alone the old man’s. The support of the dice was still enough to make the nearly impossible possible, if you got it. David almost never did.

“No. Even so, I got close, old man. I can’t believe anyone ever gets closer than this.”

It wasn’t entirely bluster on David’s part. He might not be doing a superhuman job and the dice might not be backing him up, but he had dedicated an enormous amount of good old-fashioned work to the job of getting the old man’s approval. His friends had all done significantly less, and were all walking around with classes now. It was only him that was still waiting.

“I don’t owe you anything, boy. If you were ready, you’d have my approval. You aren’t, so you don’t. It’s as simple as that.”

Months ago, David had taken that same statement at face value. Deep in the old man’s sprawling, broken-down mess of a house and stinging with defeat, he caught something in the old man’s face that made him look twice. Even so, there was just nothing there to see. The man looked patently honest, as if David would have had to explain what dishonesty was to the man before accusing him of it.

Either way, it didn’t matter. The rule was one attempt a day. It had been hard enough to convince the man to train him that David didn’t dare tempt fate beyond that. The terms were the terms.

“No luck?”

A massive shadow emerged from the raised walkway running alongside the old man’s house. David almost jumped out of his skin and whipped around to see a massive half orc woman approaching, a greataxe slung lazily across her shoulder.

“Gods, female Jasa. Announce yourself.”

“Aren’t you supposed to be a rogue? Aren’t they supposed to be attentive?

“I’m supposed to be a rogue.” David’s shoulders slumped despite his best efforts. Jasa was a good person. They had been together a long time. He still didn’t want her to see him looking as broken as he felt. “I’m supposed to have been a rogue a long time ago.”

“You really don’t think it’s your birthroll?”

“I don’t. You know it could be a one or a twenty.”

“It’s a shame, that. If I had been a thirteen, people would have known. They would have thought I’d end up being a criminal.”

“Or a thorn in the side of criminals. It goes both ways.”

“Sure. But at least we’d know. Not that we don’t know now.”

Jasa closed one of her powerful barbarian hands around his arm and yanked him around to face her. She was just a level one, but a level one barbarian was so much stronger than a normal common person like him could resist.

“You don’t know.” Her eye glinted. When she got like this, David knew that Jasa wasn’t arguing so much as she was commanding him to believe in a certain way. “Nobody does. The life of 1 is doomed. The life of a 20 is important. You can’t know which until the doom or the importance happens.”

“It’s not always doom, you know. Some ones go on to long lives of importance just like the twenties.”

“As liches, sure.” Jasa shook her head, spun David back around, and clapped him on the back. “Now come on. There’s a planning meeting at the tavern.”

“So soon?”

“Yes, so soon. We just got word from the sons of the gate today. It should be opening soon.”
